\begin{abstract}
Quick Response (QR) codes expose one public payload to any reader. StegaQR adds a
private bitstring through learned color perturbations while preserving the public
payload for a conventional reader. Its spatial bit-grid assigns each coded bit to a
cell that is upsampled to the symbol resolution. Under the same training budget, this
layout reaches full recovery at 100 bits while global message broadcast remains near
chance. Three embedding modes provide channel isolation, joint color placement, or
exact protection of QR structural modules. Across five seeds, models trained through
a stochastic photometric distortion proxy achieve 99.1 to 100 percent raw full-codeword
recovery under that proxy. On 11 held-out, non-differentiable operators, including true
JPEG and a combined resize, blur, brightness, noise, and JPEG pipeline, repetition-coded
message recovery is 100 percent in all 256 trials per operator. In a physical pilot
limited to one session, one display, and one phone, the complete pipeline of standard
detector, perspective rectifier, and neural decoder recovers both payloads from all ten
photographs (95 percent Clopper-Pearson lower bound 69 percent); perspective is removed
by the detector, not learned by the decoder. We also show that the placement of
repetition-code copies on the grid, not the code rate, decides message recovery, and
report the capacity and imperceptibility tradeoff, a module-size sweep, an architecture
ablation, and a classical baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A QR code carries a single public payload that any camera can read. That openness
drives its use in ticketing, payments, and product labeling, but it also means the
code cannot carry anything that should travel with the artifact and stay out of reach
of an ordinary scanner. A second, private channel that rides inside the same
printed or displayed code would support uses such as tamper evidence, device-pairing
secrets, and provenance tags, provided two conditions hold. A standard reader must
still decode the public payload, and the private channel must survive the way QR codes
are actually consumed, which is by photographing them from paper or a screen.

Two bodies of work come close, but each leaves a gap. Neural data-hiding methods learn
an encoder and decoder jointly and can embed a bitstring that survives printing and
re-photography~\cite{baluja2017hiding}, \cite{zhu2018hidden},
\cite{tancik2020stegastamp}, \cite{bui2023rosteals}.
They hide data inside natural photographs, however, so the recovered medium is the photo
rather than a still-scannable code. Methods that hide data inside QR codes manipulate
modules, error-correction redundancy, segment choices, or color layers by
hand~\cite{lin2016qr}, \cite{koptyra2024segmentation},
\cite{tkachenko2016twolevel}, \cite{yang2018hiq}, \cite{chu2013halftone}. Several
preserve a public payload for a standard reader, but none trains an encoder and decoder
jointly, and none is evaluated on the screen-to-camera pipeline considered here.

We present StegaQR, a method that bridges these two lines. A convolutional encoder takes
a standard QR code and a private bitstring and outputs a stego code that differs from the
original only by a learned color perturbation. A convolutional decoder recovers the
private bits from the stego code, while any conventional reader recovers the public
payload unchanged. The encoder and decoder are trained end to end through a differentiable
layer that simulates photometric corruption, compression noise, and blur. A standard QR
detector handles localization and perspective rectification before neural decoding.

The design choice that makes this work at useful payload sizes is how the private bits
enter the network. Prior neural hiders broadcast the message as a constant signal over
the whole image and read it back by global pooling. We instead lay the bits out
spatially. Each bit occupies one cell of a grid that is upsampled to the code resolution,
and the decoder pools its activation map back to the same grid to read each cell. Because
a single shared convolution then learns one write-and-read operation that applies to
every cell, learning a 100-bit code becomes about as hard as learning a handful of bits.
Under an identical training budget, the spatial layout reaches 100 percent bit accuracy
at 100 bits, while the broadcast layout stays near 52 percent, which is chance.

StegaQR exposes three switchable embedding modes that trade fault isolation, capacity,
and structural guarantees. A segregated mode uses independent per-channel sub-networks, a
cross-channel mode uses all three color channels jointly, and a QR-anchored hybrid masks
the perturbation off the finder, timing, and format modules so the structural anatomy of
the code is provably unchanged. On top of the learned channel we apply error-correction
coding, which turns the decoder's high but imperfect bit accuracy into exact message
recovery. Distortion-aware training matters: learned decoders for printed or displayed
2D barcodes are brittle to device and lighting variation, and a decoder trained for one
setting often fails in another~\cite{xie2020lcac}. Training through the distortion layer
removes this brittleness, and we confirm it on real captures.

Because the embedding strength is a tunable knob, StegaQR spans an operating frontier. At
high peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) the perturbation is not visible to a human and the
embedding is covert. At the lower PSNR needed to survive physical capture the
perturbation appears as a faint color tint, so we describe that regime as robust data
embedding rather than as human-imperceptible steganography.

This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item A neural method that embeds an arbitrary private bitstring inside a
standard-decodable QR code by learned color perturbation, distinct from hiding data in
natural images and from classical QR data hiding.
\item A spatial bit-grid payload layout, with an ablation showing it is the component
that makes the problem learnable at 100 bits (100 percent versus 52 percent for global
broadcast).
\item Three switchable embedding modes, including a QR-anchored hybrid that leaves the
structural modules untouched, compared across five seeds.
\item A distortion-trained, error-correction-coded channel characterized along the
imperceptibility, robustness, and capacity axes, with a classical baseline for reference,
and the finding that where repetition copies sit on the grid decides their recovery.
\item A pilot on real screen-to-camera capture (ten photographs, one session) in which
the private message is recovered from every photograph while the public payload stays
fully standard-decodable.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Neural data hiding in images}
Deep data hiding trains an encoder and a decoder as a pair. Baluja hid a full color
image inside a same-size cover and recovered it with a companion
network~\cite{baluja2017hiding}. HiDDeN moved to bitstring payloads and introduced the
now-standard recipe of inserting a differentiable noise layer between encoder and
decoder, so the system learns robustness to blur, cropping, and an approximation of
JPEG~\cite{zhu2018hidden}. StegaStamp carried this to the physical world: it embeds a
hyperlink bitstring into a natural photograph that stays decodable after the photo is
printed and re-captured, using simulated printing and reimaging during training and a
BCH code to clean up residual bit errors~\cite{tancik2020stegastamp}. RoSteALS placed
the payload as a single offset in the latent space of a pretrained
autoencoder~\cite{bui2023rosteals}. Two properties separate all of these from our setting:
the cover is a natural image, so the artifact that must stay useful is a photograph rather
than a scannable code, and the payload is broadcast across the whole image and read back
by global pooling, with no correspondence between a bit and a location.

\subsection{Data hiding inside QR codes}
A separate line keeps the QR code itself as the carrier. Lin and Chen embed a private
payload by exploiting QR redundancy while an ordinary scanner returns only the cover
content~\cite{lin2016qr}. Koptyra and Ogiela choose nonoptimal data segments to encode a
secret without consuming the error-correction budget; the symbols stay valid under
several readers and printed tests~\cite{koptyra2024segmentation}. The two-level QR code
replaces black modules with textured patterns recovered by correlation and Reed-Solomon
decoding~\cite{tkachenko2016twolevel}. HiQ superposes monochrome codes in color and
separates them with a trained classifier, so the composite is not a standard monochrome
QR~\cite{yang2018hiq}. Halftone QR binds the center of each submodule to the decoded
value and uses the rest for a visible image~\cite{chu2013halftone}. These methods show
that a QR symbol can carry private information, but their embedding rules are designed
by hand; StegaQR learns the perturbation and its decoder jointly against a degraded
channel.

\subsection{Robustness to the physical channel}
Robustness to printing and re-photography, and to the screen-shooting channel in which
a displayed image is captured by a camera, has become its own topic. Fang et al.
characterized screen shooting through lens deformation, light-source deformation, and
moire, and embedded the watermark redundantly at small scale~\cite{fang2019screen}.
PIMoG showed that a noise layer modeling only perspective, illumination, and moire is
enough to train a deep watermarking network end to end~\cite{fang2022pimog}. We adopt
the same principle of a differentiable distortion layer and apply it to a QR cover.

\subsection{Positioning}
StegaQR sits at an intersection that none of these occupy: a learned encoder and
decoder, like the neural image hiders, on a cover that remains standard-decodable, like
the classical QR methods. It lays the payload out spatially rather than broadcasting it,
exposes switchable modes including one that provably preserves the structural modules,
and is tested on real screen-to-camera capture. The closest neural neighbor is
StegaStamp, which is physical but uses natural-image covers; the closest QR neighbors are
the redundancy, segmentation, and textured-module schemes, which preserve a public payload
without learning the channel. Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes these contrasts.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Overview}
StegaQR turns a public payload and a private bitstring into a single stego QR image
(Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The public payload is rendered as a standard QR code, the cover.
The private bitstring is error-correction coded, arranged on a spatial grid, and fused
with the cover by a convolutional encoder that outputs a bounded color perturbation. A
standard reader recovers the public payload from the stego image directly, a convolutional
decoder recovers the private bits, and the two networks are trained jointly through a
differentiable distortion layer placed between them.

\subsection{Cover and payload representation}
\textbf{Cover.} The public payload is encoded as a version-4 QR code with error-correction
level M under ISO/IEC 18004~\cite{iso18004}, which gives a $33\times33$ module grid. Each
module is rendered as a square of $m$ pixels, so the cover is an RGB image of side $33m$;
we use $m=4$. Dark modules are black and light modules are white.

\textbf{Payload.} The private message is first expanded by an error-correcting code
(Section~\ref{ssec:ecc}) into $L$ coded bits. We place these bits on a square grid. Let
$g=\lceil\sqrt{L}\,\rceil$. The bits fill a $g\times g$ grid, one bit per cell, and the
grid is upsampled by nearest-neighbor interpolation to the cover resolution to form a
single-channel bit map $B\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}$. Each bit thus occupies a contiguous
block of pixels at a fixed location.

\subsection{Spatial bit-grid}
\label{ssec:bitgrid}
The bit map is the reason the task becomes learnable. A convolution is translation equivariant
with shared weights, so a network that learns to write one cell has learned to write every
cell, and likewise for reading. Embedding $L$ bits is then about as hard as embedding a
few. This is the point of departure from the global broadcast used by prior neural
hiders~\cite{zhu2018hidden}, \cite{tancik2020stegastamp},
\cite{bui2023rosteals}, which replicate the
message uniformly over the image and read it back by global pooling; there the network
must learn $L$ distinct global patterns, which does not scale to the payload sizes we need.
Section~\ref{ssec:ablation} quantifies the gap. Figure~\ref{fig:bitgrid} shows the write
and read paths.

\subsection{Encoder and decoder}
\textbf{Encoder.} The encoder receives the concatenation of the cover, the bit map, and,
in the hybrid mode, a structural mask (Section~\ref{ssec:modes}). It applies five
$3\times3$ convolutional blocks with 64 channels, each block being convolution, group
normalization, and ReLU with a residual connection, followed by a $1\times1$ convolution
to three channels and a $\tanh$ nonlinearity. The output is scaled by a perturbation bound
$\varepsilon$, added to the cover, and clamped:
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = \mathrm{clip}\!\big(x + \varepsilon\,\tanh(f_\theta(x,B)),\,0,\,1\big).
\end{equation}
The bound $\varepsilon$ is the single knob that trades imperceptibility against robustness.

\textbf{Decoder.} The decoder applies six convolutional blocks to the stego image, a
$1\times1$ convolution to a single-channel activation map, an adaptive average pool to the
$g\times g$ grid, and a per-cell readout that yields $L$ logits. Thresholding gives the
recovered coded bits, which the error-correcting code maps back to the message. The
architecture is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}.

\textbf{Normalization.} We use group normalization rather than batch normalization. In the
robust regime the perturbation is small relative to the cover, and the running statistics
that batch normalization accumulates during training do not match the per-batch statistics
seen at inference, which collapses decoding at test time. Group normalization removes this
train and test mismatch.

\subsection{Channel modes}
\label{ssec:modes}
The three modes share the template above but differ in how color is used and what is
protected.

\textbf{Segregated.} Three independent sub-encoders and sub-decoders, one per color
channel, each embedding $L/3$ bits on its own grid. A failure in one channel cannot
corrupt the others, which gives fault isolation at the cost of treating the channels
separately.

\textbf{Cross-channel.} A single encoder and decoder use all three channels jointly, which
gives the most freedom in placing the perturbation.

\textbf{Hybrid (QR-anchored).} The cross-channel design with two QR-aware constraints. A
binary structural mask marks the finder, timing, format, and alignment modules, and the
encoder multiplies its perturbation by this mask so those modules are left exactly as in
the cover, which guarantees the structural anatomy of the code is unchanged. Because masked
cells cannot carry payload, the hybrid layout places bits only on the data-rich cells of a
finer grid, selected once from the mask. An explicit color-calibration branch in the
decoder destabilized training without improving accuracy, so the hybrid decoder omits it;
the convolutional decoder already absorbs photometric variation.

\subsection{Distortion layer and training}
\textbf{Distortion layer.} A differentiable layer sits between the encoder and decoder
during training. It applies additive Gaussian noise, a quality-conditioned additive proxy
for compression error, brightness scaling, per-channel color shift, and Gaussian blur,
each with a fixed probability. The proxy does not perform a discrete cosine transform.
True JPEG and other non-differentiable operators are reserved for held-out evaluation.

\textbf{Objective.} The loss combines a decoding term, an imperceptibility term, and a
structural term:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_d\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}} + \lambda_p\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}}
            + \lambda_s\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{struct}}.
\end{equation}
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}}$ is the binary cross-entropy between recovered and true coded
bits. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}}$ combines mean squared error with a penalty on the largest
per-pixel deviation, keeping the perturbation small. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{struct}}$ is a
hinge that keeps structural modules close to their cover values. The hybrid decoder also
uses an auxiliary confidence loss with weight $\lambda_c=0.1$; it does not affect the
payload logits used for the reported recovery metrics.

\textbf{Curriculum.} Optimizing the decoding and imperceptibility terms together from
scratch is unstable: the imperceptibility term can drive the perturbation to zero before
the decoder has learned to read it. We therefore train decoding only for a short warmup,
then ramp the imperceptibility and structural weights linearly to their targets, with
Adam at a base rate of $10^{-3}$ under cosine annealing and gradient clipping. Checkpoint
selection maximizes the sum of raw full-codeword recovery and bit accuracy under
stochastic proxy distortion with a small PSNR tie-break, which avoids both a high-PSNR
solution that loses bits and an unnecessarily strong perturbation.

\subsection{Error-correction coding}
\label{ssec:ecc}
The decoder recovers individual bits with high but imperfect accuracy, and one wrong bit
ruins a message. We therefore protect the message with an error-correcting code before
embedding. We use a repetition code with soft-decision combining, in which the decoder sums
the logits of the repeated copies, and a Hamming(7,4) code as a higher-rate alternative.
With $k$ message bits expanded to $L$ coded bits, the net capacity is $k$ and the rate is
$k/L$. Coding converts the decoder's per-bit accuracy into exact message recovery at the
cost of capacity. Coded bit $c$ occupied grid cell $c$ in release 0.1.0 of our package;
Section~\ref{ssec:ecc_results} shows that spreading the copies of a repetition code over
the grid with a fixed cell permutation, shared by encoder and decoder, matters as much as
the code, and release 0.2.0 makes that placement the default.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Data.} Training data is generated on the fly. Each sample is a version-4,
level-M QR code rendered from a random alphanumeric public payload, paired with a random
private message. No external dataset is used. Unless stated otherwise the payload is
$L=100$ coded bits and the module size is $m=4$, giving a $132\times132$ cover. The
segregated mode uses 99 bits so its payload divides evenly across three channels.

\textbf{Training.} Each configuration of the mode comparison is trained for five seeds;
the capacity sweep uses three, the module-size sweep two, and the architecture ablation
one (Section~\ref{ssec:ablation}). We train for 24 epochs
with batch size 16, 1500 fresh samples per epoch, Adam at $10^{-3}$ with cosine
annealing and gradient clipping, a three-epoch decode-only warmup, and an eight-epoch
ramp of the imperceptibility and structural weights. We compare two regimes: a clean
regime with the distortion layer disabled, and a distortion-trained regime with it
enabled. Unless noted, the perturbation bound is $\varepsilon=0.3$ and the encoder
adapts the actual perturbation downward through the imperceptibility loss.

\textbf{Metrics.} We report bit accuracy (fraction of correct coded bits), full-decode
rate (fraction of samples with every coded bit correct), message-decode rate (fraction
of messages recovered exactly after error correction), PSNR and SSIM of the stego image
against the cover, and public decode rate, the fraction of stego images whose public
payload a standard reader (ZBar) decodes correctly. Rates are reported as mean and a 95
percent confidence interval over seeds, from 128 evaluation samples per seed unless stated
otherwise.

\textbf{Held-out operators.} Proxy-distorted evaluation uses the stochastic layer used
during training. We separately test true JPEG, PIL Gaussian blur, resize round trips,
brightness changes, sensor noise, and a combined pipeline. These operators do not
participate in gradient training.

\textbf{Hardware.} All experiments run on a single 16~GB GPU.

\subsection{The spatial bit-grid is the enabling component}
\label{ssec:ablation}
To isolate the bit-grid, we replace it with the global broadcast used by prior neural
hiders and train both under an identical budget with one seed. At 100 bits
the spatial grid reaches 100 percent bit accuracy and 100 percent full-decode, clean and
distorted, while the broadcast layout stays at 51 to 53 percent bit accuracy, at chance
level, and never achieves a single full decode. At this payload size the grid is therefore
a prerequisite for learning rather than a refinement of it.

This comparison entangles two mechanisms: a fixed location per bit, and a block of
pixels per bit that the decoder average-pools. Redundancy alone cannot be the enabler,
since the broadcast layout holds every bit at every pixel and fails. To test the
redundancy axis we retrain at fixed $L=100$ with modules of 1, 2, and 3 pixels
(Table~\ref{tab:capacity}, bottom; two seeds each), shrinking the cell from 13.2 to 3.3
pixels a side, a 16-fold drop in pixels per bit. Distorted bit accuracy stays at 99.97
to 100 percent and full-codeword recovery at 96.9 to 100 percent, the one lower value
paired with a much weaker perturbation (27.2~dB): the location, not the block, carries
the result. The distortion layer works in pixels, so its blur and noise hit small covers
harder, which biases the test against the grid. At $m=1$ the public payload is
unreadable because a 33-pixel symbol is below the reader's module-size limit; the
unembedded cover fails the same reader.

\subsection{Imperceptibility and robustness form a tunable frontier}
The perturbation bound $\varepsilon$ moves the system along a frontier
(Fig.~\ref{fig:imperceptibility}). At small $\varepsilon$ the stego code is visually
indistinguishable from the cover. A clean-trained cross-channel model reaches $54.9$~dB
PSNR with SSIM $1.000$ and full public decode, which is the covert regime
(Fig.~\ref{fig:examples}, second column). Surviving physical capture requires a larger
perturbation, which appears as a faint color tint at PSNR near $20$~dB; this is the robust
regime that we use for the capture study. Figure~\ref{fig:examples} also shows that the
hybrid residual is exactly zero on the finder patterns, confirming that the structural
modules are untouched.

\subsection{Error-correction coding and copy placement}
\label{ssec:ecc_results}
A single wrong bit ruins a message, so we measure message-decode rate after coding on
the distortion-trained cross-channel models, with 1,024 messages per seed
(Table~\ref{tab:ecc}). Hamming(7,4) recovers every message at 56 net bits. The
repetition codes expose an effect of placement rather than of rate. The repetition
encoder tiles its copies, so copy $r$ of message bit $j$ is coded bit $rk+j$, and the
grid fills row by row. With $k=20$ on the $10\times10$ grid all five copies of a bit fall
in one column, two rows apart, and the coded map becomes periodic; with $k=33$ the copies
scatter over three columns. The model is trained on independent bits, so the periodic
map lies off the training distribution and the copies fail together: for Repetition-5
the per-copy error is $0.2$ percent and the error after soft combining is $0.1$ percent,
where independent copies would give about $10^{-7}$. This is why Repetition-5 trailed
Repetition-3 and even the raw codeword. Placing the coded bits through a fixed
pseudo-random permutation of the cells, with the same weights, covers, messages, and
distortion draws, removes every failure of Repetition-2, -4, and -5 (0 of 5,120 messages
each) and all but 2 of 5,120 for Repetition-3. Hamming, whose blocks are not copies, is
unchanged, and the hybrid layout, which already scatters bits over its data-rich cells,
shows no placement effect (100 percent for every code and either placement). Release
0.2.0 of the package therefore defaults to the interleaved placement; 0.1.0 tiled copies.

\subsection{Classical baseline}
A classical per-channel least-significant-bit scheme embeds the same payload with no
training. It is perfect on clean images, at 100 percent full-decode and $78$~dB PSNR, but
collapses under proxy distortion to $53.8$ percent bit accuracy and $3.9$ percent
full-decode. The learned, distortion-trained channel closes this gap.

\subsection{Held-out non-differentiable operators}
We evaluate the released hybrid checkpoint on 256 fresh messages under each of 11
operators that are not used for gradient training. Repetition-coded message recovery is
100 percent for every operator. Raw full-codeword recovery is 100 percent for JPEG quality
70, blur radius 1, and a 50 percent resize round trip. It remains 95.3 percent for JPEG
quality 30 and blur radius 2, and 94.9 percent for the combined resize, blur, brightness,
noise, and JPEG pipeline. Error correction removes every residual message error in this
evaluation.

\subsection{Real screen-to-camera capture}
The image-operator tests do not reproduce a complete camera channel, so we also test real
captures. We displayed ten distinct stego codes from the distortion-trained hybrid model
on a monitor and photographed them with a smartphone, free-hand, at varied angle and
distance. A standard OpenCV detector localized and rectified each photograph before the
neural decoder received it. The QR was located in all ten, the public payload decoded in all ten, and the
private message, protected by a rate-1/3 code, was recovered in all ten
(Fig.~\ref{fig:capture}). Ten of ten is the observed rate; with this sample size the
two-sided 95 percent Clopper-Pearson interval has a lower bound of 69 percent, so the
pilot establishes that the pipeline works on real captures, not a deployment success
rate. It also does not claim that the neural decoder itself learns perspective
invariance: OpenCV removes the perspective, and the decoder handles the rectified
photometric channel.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The frontier makes the main limitation explicit: the covert regime, where
the perturbation is invisible, does not survive physical capture, and the regime that
survives capture carries a faint but visible color tint. We therefore present the visible
robust regime as robust data embedding rather than as human-imperceptible steganography,
and reserve the covert label for the high-PSNR end. A reader who looks closely can tell a
robust StegaQR code from a plain one. The scheme also provides no confidentiality by
itself: the private channel is gated by possession of the decoder weights, not by a key,
and the released package bundles the default model, so anyone who installs it can read
the private payload of a code produced with that model. A deployment that needs
confidentiality must train its own pair from a private seed and keep the decoder
private; that ordinary readers ignore the payload is compatibility, not security.

Net capacity is modest. At 100 embedded bits a rate-1/3 code leaves 33 message bits and a
Hamming code 56, enough for an identifier, a short token, or a key but not for a long
message. The capacity sweep shows the embedded budget extends to at least 200 bits at
high bit accuracy, so larger payloads are reachable, though exact recovery at the top of
that range leans more on the error-correcting code.

The evaluation fixes the QR version, error-correction level, and resolution, and the
physical study is a single session on one display and one camera with ten codes. These
choices are enough to establish that distortion training transfers to real capture, but a
broader sweep over devices, lighting, angle, and printed media, and over QR versions, would
strengthen the deployment case. We also do not study steganalysis: we do not measure how
hard it is for an adversary who knows the scheme to detect or remove the payload. In the
covert regime detectability is the natural next question, and in the robust regime the
relevant question is removal under adversarial post-processing.

Finally, the hybrid mode needed care to train: the structural mask removes capacity from
the protected cells, and a calibration branch we first included destabilized the cold
start. Data-rich cell placement and removing that branch resolved both; QR-aware
constraints interact with optimization in ways a plain image-hiding setup does not face.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented StegaQR, a learned method that embeds a private bitstring inside a
standard-decodable QR code through a bounded color perturbation. The design rests on a
spatial bit-grid that gives each bit a location, which lets a single shared
convolution learn the embedding at useful payload sizes where the global broadcast of prior
neural hiders stalls at chance. Three switchable modes offer different balances of fault
isolation, capacity, and structural preservation, and a QR-anchored hybrid leaves the
structural modules provably untouched. Training end to end through a differentiable
distortion proxy, and coupling the channel with error correction, yields 100 percent
message recovery across 11 held-out image operators and every one of ten photographs in
a single-session physical pilot, while a standard reader recovers the public payload in
every case.
The gains over a clean-trained model and a classical baseline are large, the
imperceptibility and robustness ends of the system form a single tunable frontier, and
the recovery of a repetition code depends on where its copies sit on the grid, not on its
rate. Code, the trained models, and a tested package are released at
\url{https://github.com/jemsbhai/stegaQR} (\texttt{pip install stegaqr}, version 0.2.0).
Future work includes larger payloads, a physical study across devices, lighting, angles,
and printed media, coverage of more QR versions, an external neural baseline retrained on
QR covers, and a steganalysis of both regimes.
